# Where a known language touches the book (exploratory, October 2026)

## 1. Zodiac centres

- Only **Pisces (f70v2)** has a Voynich word in the centre beside the Latin-script month name "mars": **otolal**
  (f70v2.33). It occurs nowhere else in the book.
- The other wheels carry the month name only. One word cannot be decoded on its own.

## 2. Last page (f116v): a Voynich word inside a German recipe line

From the full-resolution image (`images/yale/205_116v.jpg`), line 3 reads roughly:

> **oror sheey** valden/valsch vbren **so nim gas mich** o

- **oror sheey:** written in Voynich script.
- **The rest:** Latin script, German-like. "so nim …" = "then take …", the standard recipe opening; it is often read
  as "so nim geismilch", "then take goat's milk".
- The line above has Latin-script "+" separated words (michiton oladabas + multos te + … + portas + …;
  fix + marix + morix + vix + …) that read like a charm or prescription formula.

**The two Voynich words are ordinary book vocabulary:**
- **sheey** is common (149 uses).
- **oror** is rare (8 uses). In running text it is **line-final 4 of 5 times**, against an 11.8% baseline
  (binomial p ≈ 0.0009). Two of those are **paragraph-final** in the recipe section (f112v.10, `…sheey.okchedy,oror`;
  f113v.12). It also labels a plant part on f102v2.
- So **sheey and oror end a recipe paragraph together on f112v**, and on f116v the same pair is followed by
  "so nim…", "then take…".

**Caution.** "oror" was examined because it is on f116v, and the line-end pattern was noticed while looking at its
occurrences. With only 5 cases this is a lead, not a result.

**Reading (hypothesis).** Whoever wrote f116v used Voynich words in the same position as in the recipe paragraphs,
next to a German recipe instruction. That suggests the writer knew the system and used it for recipes. "oror" may be
a formula word that closes an entry; one tentative guess is something like "and so on" or "it is done".

## 3. Next steps

- Test whether other paragraph-final words in the recipe section also end lines unusually often. That would mark a
  "closing" vocabulary, a recognised feature of medieval recipe texts.
- Compare "michiton oladabas…" with known Latin charms and prescriptions.

## Addition (2026-10-04): Irish medical herbal reference

MS 23 O 6 (Royal Irish Academy, c. 1400) is a medical treatise that credits **Uilliam Ó hÍceadha** (O'Hickey family) with translating herbal material. It describes conditions and their cures in the same format as medieval herbals: symptom → cause → cure. This provides a direct reference for understanding how the O'Hickeys structured medical knowledge, and how the Voynich's herbal and recipe sections may follow the same organizational logic.
